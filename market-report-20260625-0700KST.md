The session's twin narratives—AI memory demand forcing consumer hardware cost pass-through versus Micron's confirmation that the AI memory supercycle is accelerating—set up a cautious but constructive close to the trading week on Friday.

## 2. MAJOR NEWS, EVENTS & ANNOUNCEMENTS

### A. Apple and Microsoft Announce Consumer Product Price Hikes — Mag7 Selloff

The dominant market theme of June 25 was a sharp selloff in mega-cap consumer technology, led by **Apple (AAPL)** and **Microsoft (MSFT)** announcing price increases on consumer hardware—both companies explicitly citing spiraling semiconductor costs driven by the AI data center buildout.

**Apple (AAPL, −~5.3%):** Apple announced price increases across select MacBook and iPad lineups, with certain models rising by up to $300. The iPad Air 128GB now starts at $749 (up from $599), while the iPad Pro Wi-Fi 256GB rose from $999 to $1,199. The company confirmed iPhone prices remain unchanged for now. The stock fell approximately 5.3% on the session, erasing **~$275 billion in market capitalization** and bringing Apple's total market value to just over $4 trillion—the largest single-day market cap destruction for Apple in 2026.

The magnitude of Thursday's decline follows an initial modest reaction on June 24 (−0.74%) when the iPad/MacBook increases were first announced; Thursday's session saw the full market reassessment as analysts across the Street published detailed notes on margin impact and demand elasticity. APAC Research and KGI Securities both maintained Sell-equivalent ratings. Goldman Sachs, maintaining its Buy with a $340 price target, argued the increases would "materially improve gross margin trajectories in consumer hardware" even if unit demand softens. However, Wedbush's Dan Ives flagged that May iPhone shipments in China were already down 19% year-over-year—with Apple's Chinese market share falling to approximately 11%, a multi-year low—making demand elasticity risk in Apple's most critical growth market very real.

Apple's weighting in the Nasdaq (~12%) and the S&P 500 (~7%) made the stock the single largest drag on both indices. The Apple slide also signals a structurally uncomfortable dynamic: the AI buildout that is minting record profits at chip companies is simultaneously squeezing the consumer hardware companies that depend on those chips.

**Microsoft (MSFT, −3.78%):** Microsoft announced Xbox console price increases of $100–$150 per model, effective August 1, also attributing the hikes to "elevated memory and storage chip costs." The announcement added to tech sector pressure and underscored that the AI hardware cost shock is not limited to a single consumer tech name.

Morgan Stanley published a note mid-session: *"The coordinated price hike announcements from Apple and Microsoft represent the clearest evidence yet that the AI infrastructure supercycle is transmitting cost pressure asymmetrically—upstream chip and memory producers benefit enormously; downstream consumer hardware companies absorb costs they cannot fully pass through without demand destruction."*

### B. May PCE Inflation: In-Line Headline, Slight Core Miss — Bond Market Relief

The Bureau of Economic Analysis released the May Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) price index—the Federal Reserve's preferred inflation gauge—providing the session's key macro data:

| Metric | Actual | Consensus | Prior (April) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Headline PCE (YoY) | +4.1% | +4.1% | +3.8% |
| Core PCE (YoY) | +3.4% | +3.3% | +3.1% |
| Personal Spending (MoM) | +0.7% | +0.6% | +0.4% |

The headline reading exactly matched consensus—the highest reading since April 2023—providing modest bond market relief by removing the tail risk of a significant upside surprise that would have forced the Fed to consider a September rate hike. Core PCE's slight upside miss (+3.4% vs. +3.3% expected) was insufficient to materially shift Fed rate expectations. The 10-year Treasury yield fell 4 basis points to 4.45%, its second consecutive session of declines.

Key inflation drivers included: (1) sustained energy price pressure from the Iran conflict's impact on global oil markets; (2) rising AI-related computer component costs; and (3) strong services inflation across restaurants, hotels, auto repair, and healthcare. Separately, personal spending's +0.7% monthly rise (above the +0.6% estimate) signals that the consumer remains resilient despite elevated prices—a mixed signal for the Fed, as strong spending both validates the economy's health and sustains demand-driven inflation.

**Federal Reserve context:** At the June 17 FOMC meeting—Chair Kevin Warsh's first—the committee unanimously held the fed funds rate at 3.50%–3.75%. The June dot plot was hawkish: the median year-end 2026 projection shifted to 3.80% (up from 3.4% in March), implying at least one 25-basis-point hike. Nine of 18 members project at least one hike; eight expect no change; one sees a cut. Thursday's PCE print did not materially alter October as the market's base-case meeting for the first hike, with fed funds futures implying approximately 68–70% probability of a year-end hike after the data.

### S&P 500 — 7,357.49 (−0.01%)

The index ended essentially flat, concealing an extreme dispersion of outcomes beneath the surface. The **Technology** sector was the primary drag: Apple's ~5.3% decline (second-largest S&P weight) and Microsoft's 3.78% drop (third-largest) combined to subtract an estimated 55–65 basis points from the index on their own. This drag was almost entirely offset by gains in **Industrials** (Caterpillar +6%), **Health Care** (J&J +~1%), and the semiconductor-driven subset of Technology (Qualcomm +10%, Micron +18.1%).

Sector bifurcation was the sharpest of 2026 on a single session basis: Consumer Discretionary and Information Technology underperformed materially while Industrials, Health Care, and Financials outperformed. The equal-weight S&P 500 meaningfully outperformed the cap-weight index—the clearest signal that the session's weakness was concentrated in a handful of multi-trillion-dollar names, not a broad market deterioration.

JPMorgan maintained its 12-month S&P 500 target of 8,200 in a post-PCE note, arguing that AI-earnings growth remains the dominant secular driver and that Apple/MSFT's cost-pass-through announcements are "idiosyncratic hardware cost management, not macro deterioration." ### What Drove Rate Moves on June 25

The Treasury market saw a modest but broad-based rally (yields lower across all maturities) following the May PCE release. The headline reading exactly matching consensus removed the tail risk of a hot upside surprise that would have pulled forward rate hike expectations. While core PCE's slight miss (+3.4% vs. +3.3% est.) prevented a more aggressive bond rally, the net read from the data was that the Fed's current pause is sustainable through at least early Q4.

The 10-year yield's second consecutive session of declines—from ~4.51% earlier this week to 4.45%—also reflects the carry-over impact of Wednesday's oil price collapse. Lower energy prices reduce inflation expectations embedded in long-duration yields, and while oil prices stabilized Thursday, the prior session's dramatic 4%+ decline continues to filter through breakeven inflation rates (10-year breakevens fell an estimated ~4 basis points on Wednesday and held lower Thursday).

**Federal Reserve policy context:** Chair Kevin Warsh held rates unanimously at 3.50%–3.75% at the June 17 FOMC meeting, the second consecutive hold. The updated dot plot shows a median year-end 2026 rate projection of 3.80%, with nine of 18 members projecting at least one hike and six projecting two. The Fed raised its 2026 PCE inflation forecast to 3.6% (from 2.7% in March) and trimmed GDP growth to 2.2%. ### USD/JPY — 161.84 (+0.11%)

The yen remains near multi-decade lows, with USD/JPY advancing to 161.84 on Thursday. The fundamental driver remains the wide interest rate differential: the Bank of Japan holds its policy rate near 0.50% while the US Fed funds rate sits at 3.50%–3.75%—a spread of approximately 300 basis points. Despite this, the BoJ has not acted forcefully to raise rates or defend the yen, viewing domestic inflation dynamics as insufficient justification for aggressive normalization.

Japanese Ministry of Finance officials have issued periodic verbal warnings about "excessive and one-sided" yen movements, but no formal intervention occurred on June 25. Market participants remain alert to the 163–165 range as a potential trigger for coordinated action; at 161.84, the pair is uncomfortably close to those levels. The pair's 40-year lows have significant political dimensions given elevated domestic inflation pass-through from import costs.

**Cross-asset implications:**
- **Yen carry trade:** The wide USD/JPY spread sustains structural demand for yen-funded carry trades (borrow cheap in yen, buy US Treasuries or EM assets). A sudden reversal—via BoJ surprise or intervention—would trigger a disorderly unwind similar to August 2024, representing the most acute systemic FX risk in markets.
- **Japanese equities (Nikkei):** Yen weakness at these levels provides tailwind for Japanese export company revenues in yen terms, supporting Nikkei performance.
- **Gold:** Dollar strength at 14-month highs and positive real yields (~+1.05% on 10yr vs. core PCE) cap gold's upside despite geopolitical risk from the Iran situation.
- **Emerging markets:** Dollar strength continues to pressure EM currencies and USD-denominated sovereign debt, with countries carrying significant dollar liabilities facing growing refinancing pressure as the higher-for-longer regime extends.
